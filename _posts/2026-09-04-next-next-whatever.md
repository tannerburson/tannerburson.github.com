Figuring out how to "get to the next level" is a topic I've faced personally and coached folks on for years. I've often used Andrew Chen's [Next Next Job](https://andrewchen.com/the-next-next-job/) as a starting point. The specific advice has aged, but the core idea is still sound: career decisions work best with a view past the current one. Where he loses me is the assumption that everyone can, and wants to, generate a pile of competing job offers to evaluate as the only means of growth. That's never been true for most of us, and it definitely isn't in today's market. Here's how I've been using this framing for a while.

## What's after next?

The question of "how to get to the next level" comes from a good place, but it's too narrow and its horizon too short to uncover the bigger opportunities. Instead of asking "what's next," I ask "what feels _next next_, what's after the next thing?" and then dig into _why_ it feels further out. The timeline is deliberately vague. This isn't the maligned "where do you see yourself in 5 years." It might be a year out, it might be a lot more. What matters, and what eases the pressure, is that it's not _the next thing_.

The question is about what you want to be *able to do*, not which title you want. I believe the most _valuable thing we have in tech careers is the near-infinite opportunity to learn_. I know it's time to push when I don't feel like I'm learning. That's when I re-run this exercise to figure out what I should be learning, doing, or taking on next.

## Gaps vs. strengths

When people run this exercise, they almost uniformly focus on gaps: "I don't know enough front-end", "I haven't led a multi-quarter initiative", "I haven't mentored anyone". Rarely does anyone lead with a strength and lean into it. But strengths are what others value most in us, and they're where we spend the least intentional development time. Be intentional about **which gaps are disqualifying**, the ones that will actually block the "next next whatever," and **which strengths could accelerate your path there**. Gaps are defense. Strengths are offense. And it's usually _more fun_ to sharpen a strength than to fill a gap.

## More than one target

I've never had only a single path on my list. There are usually several, sometimes in direct opposition. Aim at multiple targets and you'll hit none of them, but ranking what's most interesting _now_, and knowing what might nudge you toward an alternate path, is still worth doing. This shows up often as "technical leadership vs. management." People have a bias toward one side (I certainly have, at different points) and get hung up on how to move that direction.

Twice I set out to be a non-managing technical leader and wound up with a growing managerial scope. Not because I'd ranked "manager" at the top of my list, but because the biggest opportunities I could take on, where I was, required me to manage. It went well once and less well once, and both shaped how I think about the leader/manager split. That's the value of writing the target down. Not that you'll be right (I'm often wrong about what I end up wanting). It's that being intentional makes it obvious when you've changed course, and much easier to judge whether the trade was worth it.

## Growth != New Job

I've had as many chances to change my trajectory inside a company as I've had by changing jobs. My path to where I am was not a string of 18-30 month job hops. It came from asking hard questions about what I wanted, then taking a risk on a new project, a new team, or a new tech stack where I already was. Most of those went well, a few didn't, and I learned from all of them. 

Re-evaluating what was important to me never _required_ a competing offer, a new title, or a promotion. It started with looking at where I was, what I wanted, and what options might be available. Eventually you can hit the end of the runway and it's time to look elsewhere, but it rarely starts there.

## What to do

Write this down, as quickly as you can. Then put it away for a few days and come back to it.

> Ask yourself not what's next, but what's the step beyond that. Why does it feel an extra step away? What skills, knowledge, and experiences do you need before it can feel like the next step? How do you need to position yourself to get there? What strengths can you build into superpowers that accelerate it?

Write a second or third version with a different target, then see which feels more natural and exciting.

And then talk to someone about it. A manager, a mentor, a friend, anyone who can sharpen your thinking.

And then put it into action: join the new team, ask for the problem no one wants, highlight your plan for the looming cliff. 

Head toward your Next Next Whatever.
